Bringing methodologies and learnings from other disciplines, like UX, can provide high value to Data projects. In this article, I'll go through my recent collaboration with UX Designers, where we worked together in the creation of a dashboard for our Pharmacy Operations team.

We needed to define a set of metrics that helped the PO team to have better visibility of their operations, and understand where should they put their efforts. This is, tracking the time it takes to process orders, identify bottlenecks, track reasons for cancellations, etc.

This is the first time I collaborate with UX, since the usual approach consists on me talking directly to the stakeholders to gather requirements, asking why they need the data and how are they going to drive impact from that, and iterating over it. 

The experience of collaborating with them proved to be incredibly insightful, reinforcing my belief in the power of integrating ideas from diverse disciplines into Data projects. Here are some key insights I gained:


# Crafting a narrative with data
Data becomes insightful when it tells a compelling story, a skill at which UX designers excel. Their expertise lies in visual storytelling, simplifying complex information and ensuring an intuitive user experience. They understand how the flow of actions in a website should be, taking into account the type of customer that will visit it.

A dashboard should be easily interpretable and it should be designed in a way that it easily guides the user to help figure out what is going right and what is going wrong. Even first time users should find it easy to grasp the core ideas that the dashboard is trying to tell. That's why we have to be constantly asking: "what is this dashboard telling me and what am I going to do with this information?"

Moreover, creating a dashboard should involve user research. It is not only about identifying which metrics are better to display and why. It involves understanding the type of user you have, and doing an attempt to put in their shoes and understanding if what is being displayed makes sense. One of the worst things that can happen is that people take the wrong decisions based on a misinterpretation of the data! Showing the dashboard to someone that hasn't seen it before and asking them to explain what they see is a good practice. This links very well with the next point:


# Providing context
Context is essential in data interpretation. Without a baseline for comparison, numbers lack significance. "Number of orders delivered this month" doesn't mean anything if we don't compare with a target or historical data. Comparisons helps us tell a story and set up a context of expectations. We need benchmarks.

Without providing context, how will you know whether those numbers are good or bad, or if they are typical or unusual? Without comparison values, numbers on a dashboard are meaningless for the users. And more importantly, they won’t drive any action and it will just reinforce the mindset of "the more data the better".

Context is also essential in the sense of being clear on what we are showing in a dashboard.
Most stakeholders don’t work with data every day, and most of the times they don’t know what they are looking at or what to do when they go to the dashboards. 

Always try to provide maximum information, even if some of them seem obvious to you, your audience might find them perplexing. Name all the axes and measurement units, and add titles to all charts.

Should dashboards include context of what they mean and what they should be used to? I believe that they can do part of the job, but they can be limited in educating stakeholders.

I believe that a big part of the education comes from the data team. The data team should encourage an analytical mindset in stakeholders. People should come to dashboards and start asking the WHYs. Why did we have lower sales this month? That, in consequence, triggers an analysis to find the root of the problem. 

A good data team should be able to balance two skills: educating others in data and present data that doesn't need explanation. Someone told me once that a good product doesn't need a guide on how to use it, it should be intuitive. In the data world that's difficult to accomplish, but we should strive for a balance.


# The aesthetics of information
Colour palettes and graph spacing play a pivotal role in creating visually appealing dashboards. I wouldn't have thought that a light palette of colours and proper spacing between graphs would make such a difference in making a dashboard pleasant to the eyes.


# The art of semantics
One of the most difficult skills to master in data modelling is semantics. How do we name a table? How do we name a column? Semantics are more an art than a science. There is no general rule on how something should be named because it greatly depends on the context. However, we can always attempt to define a set of best practices or methods to at least attempt to do a good job at data modelling.

Copywriting is a discipline that can bring a lot of valuable learnings to the data modelling field. 

A copywriter is someone who writes with the intent of educating, persuading or influencing their audience, often with the goal of selling a product or service. This skill can help to help define how metrics should be named and how they should be described in the data catalogue. 

I believe that copywriting skills are useful for an Analytics Engineer, Data Scientist, Data person... Even Software Engineers would benefit from this, since one of the biggest complains in code development is lack of documentation or bad documentation.


# Simplicity
Simplicity is one of my top principles. 
Humans are limited and can only focus their attention on a few things at a time.

It's easy to fall in the fear of missing out. We want to track as many metrics as possible.

However, the fewer metrics we have, the easier it is to focus the conversation on what is important, start asking the correct WHYs and making assumptions to find the root cause of whatever problem we are having.


# Conclusion
After working closely with UX, I believe that the idea of bringing ideas from other disciplines to data can be very powerful.

I'm eager to learn more about core practices in UX, UI and Copywriting, which I believe will help me to become a better professional. Writing posts like this one is (slowly) helping me to be a better writer, so that's a start!
