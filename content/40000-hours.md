SUMMARY TLDR!

Recently, I've completed reading Four Thousand Weeks by Oliver Burkeman, a book that I found highly engaging and insightful. While it has some ideas that I've already seen in other similar books, it had pieces of insight that truly resonated with me. This book made some concepts 'click' in my mind and made me realise about certain unhealthy mindsets that I did not realise I had. 

Since I believe this book has played a role in changing my perspective, I wanted to make a summary, for myself and anyone reading this, of the main ideas I've collected, so I can look back at them from time to time:


# You will never complete your To Do list
As a to-do list addict, I always have a list of things to do that I keep reorganising almost every day. Sometimes, this even evolves into a multitude of lists that at some point I merge back into one, only to find myself having multiple lists a few days later.

Many times I've found myself anxious for never completing all the tasks I have on that list.
I thought that I had to complete everything there to have the feeling of having had a productive day, but I never stopped to think and embrace the fact that this would never happen.

This book has helped me to acknowledge that I will always have something on my list of things to do. For every problem solved, three more show up. That's how it works, we are never certain that solving a certain problem will uncover another one, we can't read the future and we have to accept it.

This reduced the pressure I put on myself to find out that, by the end of the day, I still had tasks to do.

I found this advice interesting: If you've decided that you will work on a given project for fifty minutes, stop when the time is complete and move to another topic. Why? Because the desire to keep going comes from the impatience of not having finished. Of not being productive enough. Stopping when you have to stop, helps to make the muscle of patience stronger, to maintain a level of productivity and reduce burnout.


# On behaving like we will live forever
Sometimes we live and behave like we can do everything we want to do. Like we will live forever.
However, it's important to realise that the things we can potentially do are limitless, but the things we can do are limited.

Doing anything requires a sacrifice: the sacrifice of not doing all the other things you could be doing. If you never ask yourself if the sacrifice is worth it, your days will be full of more and more trivial and tedious things, things that haven't been judged if they are more important than others.

The more experiences you have, the more experiences you think you could be living, which ends up in frustration.
Therefore, start behaving like someone who accepts his time in this world is limited and plan your days knowing that, without any doubt, you won't have time to do everything you want to do. Don't mortify yourself for not doing everything. Embrace that you will only live a bunch of experiences and enjoy them as much as you can.

Most of the time, you shouldn't hold a decision until the moment you are 'prepared'. You will never be prepared, and your time is not unlimited, so you have to act despite the feeling of not being prepared.

It's easy to go through the years as if your life was a rehearsal for the big play and justify it saying that what you are doing now is acquiring new abilities and experiences that will allow you to have control over things in the future. But you need to act even if you think you are not 100% prepared, you'll never be 100% prepared.


# Not everything we do has to be productive
Sometimes we act as if everything we do only has a value if it sets the foundation of something else. Not everything has a benefit for your future self. You can enjoy a walk without needing to listen to that podcast to feel productive. Just enjoy the walk.


# On the uncomfortable sensation of doing hard things
To start working on a difficult project, we don't have to actively look for peace or focus but recognise that discomfort is inevitable and that it is better to acknowledge the reality of the situation than to rebel against it.


# On planning for the future
Many times we behave as if we have some control over the future. However, you have no way of knowing if things will turn out as you want them to. This doesn't mean that you shouldn't do your best to be prepared for a future scenario or situation, not at all. It means that you have little control over which external factors will affect your plans, so don't torture yourself if things don't go as planned.

The things you value most in your life always originate in a combination of random events that you couldn't have planned for, and that you certainly can't retrospectively change now. And if you go way back, before you were born, the accumulation of coincidences is even more overwhelming.

Live a life without demanding that the future will adapt to what you want from it. Again, this doesn't mean that we should act prudently in the present to reduce the chances of something bad occurring in the future.

Our obsession with making the most of our time in the future prevents us from seeing that the moment of truth is always now, that life is nothing more than a succession of presents that culminate in death, and that you will probably never have all your affairs in perfect order. And, therefore, it is best that you stop postponing the "true meaning" of your existence and start living now.

The discomfort you feel when you try too hard to be "more in the present" has to do with the mental discomfort of trying to do something impossible: modify your relationship with the present moment in time, when, in fact, that moment in time is, after all, all you are. «We can't get anything out of life. There's nothing outside where we can take anything. There is no pocket outside of life [in which we can] snatch supplies from life and store them. There is no place outside the life you have right now. Living more fully in the present may just be a matter of finally realizing that you've never had a choice but to be here now.


# Your time is not your time
You can't think you have control over your time because you don't. You think you are entitled to '80 years' of life but you have no right the be alive in the first place. Acknowledge that you don't have control over how you will spend your time in a single day.


# On enjoying having problems
Behind our eagerness to overcome all obstacles or challenges as quickly as possible, in an attempt to have them "solved", there is usually tacitly hidden the fantasy that a day will come when we will not suffer any type of problems. As a result, most of us act as if the problems we encounter are doubly problematic: first, because of the problem itself; and second, because we believe, at least subconsciously, that we should not have problems. And yet, it is evident that we will never stop having them. In fact, you should never want to stop having them, because in a life without problems, there would be nothing worth doing and therefore it would be empty of meaning. Because what is a “problem” really? The most generic definition is that it is something that requires your attention, and if life did not come with those types of demands, nothing would make sense. When you give up the unattainable goal of wanting to eradicate all your problems, it is possible to come to appreciate the fact that life is nothing more than facing one problem after another, giving each one the time it needs; in other words, that problems are not an impediment to a meaningful existence, but rather its very substance.


# On free time
The value of time proceeds not from how much you have, but from whether it is in sync with the time of the people you most care about. That's why the tendency to want more free time for ourselves might not mean more happiness.


# On taking ourselves less seriously
When something seems to surpass you, what better consolation than to remember that everything is, as long as you are willing to contemplate it with a certain perspective, indistinguishable from the most absolute nothingness? Everything that turns any existence upside down—relationship problems, status rivalries, financial worries—instantly becomes irrelevant.

What you do with your life doesn't matter much, and as far as your limited time is concerned, the universe doesn't care at all how you spend it.

Overestimating the importance of your existence causes you to have an unrealistic idea of what it means to spend your limited time well. It sets the bar too high. It suggests that to count as “well spent”, your life must include great achievements or produce a lasting impact on future generations.


# On a less boring life
More experiences lead to even wanting more experiences which leads to frustration.
If we start being more present and finding new experiences in our day-to-day tasks, life can be lived with more intensity. In other words, stay present. Choose another route to work, start doing photography, painting... Not everything has to be about travelling to far and expensive places.


# On the uncertainty of dealing with other people's actions
Curiosity is an attitude that adapts well to the inherent unpredictability of human nature because it is helpful if people act the way you want and if they don't. The position of demanding a person act a certain way can result in frustration if they don't go as planned.


# Favourite quote
Accepting your limits means abandoning the hope that, with the right methods and a little more effort, you will be able to take on the limitless demands of others, realize all your ambitions, excel in every area of your life or give to all just causes or humanitarian crises the attention they seem to deserve. It means giving up hope that you will ever feel like you have everything under control, or that you will not have deeply painful experiences. And it means abandoning, as much as possible, the main hope behind all of this: that nothing is real, that this is nothing more than a rehearsal and there will come a day when you will truly be safe. That you will have what it takes.

When you start to internalize it, even just a little, what you feel is not discouragement, but an invigorating shot of motivation. You realize that the terrible eventuality that you had subliminally been preparing for your whole life, because it was going to be a horrible experience, has already happened, and you are still here, you are still alive, at least for the moment. You realize that you have never really needed the feeling of complete security that you had been trying so desperately to achieve. It's liberating. When you no longer need to convince yourself that the world is not full of uncertainty and tragedy, you are free to focus on doing what you can to help. And when you no longer need to convince yourself that you will do all the things you want to do, you are free to focus on a few things that are worthwhile.
